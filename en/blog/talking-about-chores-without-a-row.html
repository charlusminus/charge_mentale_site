How to talk about sharing the chores without it ending in a row

10 November 2026 · 3 min read

A method that takes one evening, tried in our home and with friends: the same list, each of you on your own, then the comparison. Why to start with the facts, what not to say, and what to do with the result.

Most conversations about sharing the chores get off to a bad start: on a tired evening, about one specific thing that hasn't been done, with a sentence that begins ‘you never’. They usually end with a list of examples on each side, and with nothing. We have had those conversations. Then we found a way to have them differently, and it fits into one evening.

Why it goes wrong

Two reasons, and neither has anything to do with bad faith.

The first is that you are not talking about the same home. In a YouGov survey of men and women living with a partner of the other sex, published in August 2022, 63% of women said cooking at home was mostly or entirely up to them, but only 48% of men said their partner did most or all of it. For washing clothes, it was 77% of women against 55% of men. Among parents, 61% of fathers said childcare was split evenly; only 38% of mothers agreed. In most couples there is a gap between what one of you thinks you do and what the other thinks you do. Until that gap is on the table, you are arguing about two different realities.

The second is that you talk about one task in order to talk about everything. The forgotten load of washing is never really the subject; the subject is the feeling of carrying the whole household. But you cannot answer a feeling with a load of washing. You need a complete picture first, and only then a conversation.

The method, in one evening

It takes an hour, the same list for both of you, and not talking until the end.

Each on your own, first. Each of you fills in the list alone, without looking at the other's: for every task, who looks after it and who thinks of it. Fifteen minutes. We borrowed the idea from a French couples' worksheet: the gap between the two versions is a result in itself. On the site, each of you can take stock on your own, then compare.

Compare, without commenting. Put the two results side by side. Look first at the lines where you agree: often that is most of them, and it is reassuring straight away. Then the lines where you don't. At this stage, nobody is right. You simply note them.

Look at the three figures. Time per day for each of you, the number of tasks each of you does, the number of tasks each of you keeps in mind. These are three different questions. A couple can be at 55/45 on time and 90/10 on what is kept in mind. That third figure is usually the one behind the feeling of carrying the household, and it is the one nobody had ever talked about.

Choose three lines, not thirty. The result covers about fifty tasks. You won't change fifty. Choose three, the ones that weigh the most or where your answers differ the most, and decide who takes each of them in full, including thinking of it. The rest can wait for next time. Our article on delegating without the thinking explains why ‘in full’ matters.

Set the next time. In six months. With the same list. Without that, the conversation turns back into a row on a tired evening.

The sentences that derail it

‘You never’ and ‘I do everything’ are untrue in practically every case, and the other person knows it, so that is all they respond to. Taking stock makes them unnecessary: you have the figures.

‘You should've asked’ too, from the other side. Asking is work, as the cartoonist Emma showed in a comic of that name. The right answer to ‘you didn't tell me’ is not ‘you should have noticed’, it is to hand over the task in full, thinking included.

And ‘other couples’. The national averages are there to help you understand, not to measure your relationship against. The couple next door doesn't live with your hours. If you are curious about them all the same, here they are: the Office for National Statistics estimated in 2016 that women did on average 60% more unpaid work than men (26 hours a week against 16, using 2015 data), and its March 2023 survey found that employed women aged 18 to 64 spent 2 hours 37 minutes a day on unpaid household work, against 2 hours 2 minutes for men. Useful context, not a yardstick.

What to do with the result if it is uneven

It probably will be. An uneven result is not an accusation, it is a starting point. The question to ask is not ‘how do we get to 50/50?’ but ‘is this the gap we want?’ There can be very good reasons for wanting a gap, for instance when one of you works much longer paid hours, a question we come back to in paid work and housework. There is never a good reason not to know about it.

What helped us most was taking stock at a calm moment, not after a row. A Sunday afternoon, with a pot of tea, when nobody has anything to hold against anybody. The result is a picture of where things stand, not a trial. The day it becomes a trial, you have lost what it had to offer. When you are ready, you can take stock at home, free and with no sign-up.
