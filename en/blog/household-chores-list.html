A household chores list for two: fifty tasks, and what each one takes to think of

28 November 2026 · 3 min read

Shopping, meals, cleaning, laundry, children, paperwork, home: the regular tasks of a household, and for each one what has to be kept in mind before anyone starts.

A household runs on about fifty regular tasks, in ten families: shopping, meals, cleaning, laundry, the children's day to day, their school, health and activities, paperwork and money, planning family life, the home with its garden and vehicles, and pets if you have any. Every task has two parts: doing it, and thinking of it. Here is the list used by the Who thinks of it? test, with what has to be kept in mind for each task before anyone even starts.

What are the household chores?

Shopping and supplies

  • Make the shopping list: notice what is running out and what is going off, plan the week's meals.
  • Do the shopping: the shop, the market or click and collect, carrying, putting it all away.

Meals

  • Take care of the meals: choose what to eat, cook breakfast, lunch and dinner.
  • Clear the table and do the washing-up: dishwasher or by hand.

Cleaning and tidying

  • Tidy away what's lying around: toys, belongings, post, worktop. Every day.
  • Clean the kitchen: worktop, hob, sink. Every day too.
  • Do the dusting and do the floors: hoover, broom, mop.
  • Take care of the bins: put them out, bring them in, change the bags, recycling.
  • Clean the bathroom and toilet.
  • Change the sheets and towels.
  • Sort out and give away what's no longer used: clothes that are too small, toys, the loft, seasonal things.
  • Do the deep clean: windows, fridge, oven, cupboards, a few times a year.

Laundry

  • Put the washing on: sort, treat stains, hang out or tumble dry.
  • Do the ironing and put the clean washing away.
  • Look after the clothes: mending, dry cleaner's, shoe repairs, alterations.
  • Wash the bulky items: duvets, pillows, curtains, a few times a year.

Take stock at home

Fifty tasks already listed, two questions for each: who does it, who thinks of it. Ten minutes together, free, no sign-up.

Take stock

Children: day to day

  • Get the children ready in the morning, do the drop-off at school, nursery or the childminder's, and the pick-up in the evening.
  • After-school snack, homework, end of the day, then bath time, teeth and bedtime.
  • Look after the baby: breastfeeding or bottles, baby food, nappies.
  • Take care of the nights: a child who wakes, a nightmare, a bottle, a poorly child.
  • Play, go out, read with the children.
  • Look after the children when there is no school, nursery or childminder.
  • Look after a poorly child: staying at home, the GP, medicine.

Children: school, health, activities

  • Keep up with school admin: the school app, emails, parents' evenings, trips, forms to sign, supplies.
  • Keep track of the children's health: GP, dentist, vaccinations, prescriptions, glasses.
  • Manage the children's activities: signing up, timetables, kit, getting them there.
  • Manage the children's clothes: sizes, seasons, shoes.
  • Organise childcare: childminder, nursery, after-school club, grandparents, school holidays.
  • Organise birthday parties, theirs and their friends'.
  • Set and keep the rules: screens, sleep, the difficult conversations.
  • Keep the family memories: photos, drawings, the red book.

Paperwork and money

  • Manage the household money: bills, accounts, budget, savings.
  • Deal with the admin and paperwork: contracts, insurance, bank, council tax, tax and benefits.
  • Keep the household tech working: broadband, phones, passwords, subscriptions.
  • Be the employer of the nanny or home help: contract, payroll, cover.

Planning, family, friends

  • Keep the family calendar and plan the weekends: who is where, when, and what you are doing on Sunday.
  • Keep in touch with friends and family: invitations, calls, visits, news.
  • Think of presents and celebrations: birthdays, Christmas, cards, thank-yous.
  • Organise the holidays: choosing, booking, packing.
  • Help an elderly, ill or disabled loved one: appointments, shopping, admin, visits.

Home, outdoors, vehicles

  • Repair, do the DIY, oversee the building work: a light bulb, a leak, painting, tradespeople.
  • Look after the plants, garden or balcony: watering, mowing, pruning.
  • Look after the vehicles: MOT, service, tyres, insurance.
  • Keep the house maintained: boiler, chimney sweep, smoke alarms.
  • Manage a second home or a rented property, if you have one.

Pets

  • Feed and walk the pets: bowls, litter tray, walks.
  • Keep track of their health: vet, vaccinations, care during the holidays.

Why is a chores list not enough?

A list says what there is to do. It doesn't say who thinks of it. Yet most tasks begin long before anyone starts on them: noticing there is no milk left, knowing the booster jab is due in March, seeing that the shoes have become too small. The French sociologist Monique Haicault described this ‘ordinary management of life for two’ as early as 1984. In 2019, Allison Daminger broke it down into four steps: anticipating, identifying the options, deciding, and checking it has been done.

Official statistics only see the hours. In the UK, the Office for National Statistics found in 2016 that women did 60% more unpaid work than men, 26 hours a week against 16. That says nothing about who keeps track, which is what the mental load is.

This is why the test asks two questions for each task: who does it, and who thinks of it. The two don't always go together. The person who writes the shopping list is not necessarily the one who pushes the trolley, and handing over a task while keeping the thinking lightens very little.

How do you use this list as a couple?

You can print it and go through it together. You can also take stock together on the site: for each task, you say who does it and who thinks of it, one of you, the other, both, or neither because it doesn't apply to you. The result gives the hours per day and the number of things each of you keeps in mind. It takes about ten minutes, it is free, and there is no sign-up.

The suggested times are averages. You can correct them task by task under the result, and add a task that is missing in your home: the allotment, the log burner, the school run by car. You can fill it in together, or each on your own before comparing.

The result doesn't point at anyone. It makes visible what gets done, and what gets thought of, so you can talk about it on the same footing.

Where does this list come from?

It follows the broad categories of national time-use surveys (cooking, cleaning, laundry, shopping, childcare, household management, DIY and gardening), completed with Eve Rodsky's Fair Play, whose hundred cards spell out everything that keeps a home running. The wording is deliberately generic: every household has its own habits, and those are adjusted in the detail of each task. What couples who have taken stock found is published on the figures page.
