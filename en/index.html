Why take stock

Most couples have never listed everything there is to do. Each of you sees what you do, and part of what the other does. The rest is invisible: noticing that the washing powder has run out, knowing when the dentist appointment is, checking that the swimming bag is ready. That work does not take hours, it takes up room in your head.

This page judges nothing. It puts figures in place of impressions, so that the conversation starts from something concrete. In our home, the first version mostly revealed the tasks nobody saw.

Frequently asked questions

What is the mental load?

The constant work of managing, organising and planning the smooth running of the home, as defined by Nicole Brais. In practice: noticing that something needs doing, planning it, checking that it is done, even when someone else carries it out. The term comes from the French sociologist Monique Haicault (1984). Allison Daminger (2019) showed that anticipating and monitoring almost always fall to the same person in the couple, even though decisions are made together.

How is the result calculated?

For each task, you say who does it and who thinks of it (thinking of it also means having to ask or remind). Hours per day are based on ‘who does it’: number of times per period, multiplied by the duration, brought back to a single day. The mental load is measured in things to keep in mind, not in hours: no serious survey converts thinking into minutes. A task you think of every week or more counts three times, every month twice, less often once. The default durations come from the INSEE time-use survey in France (2010) and the Fair Play card deck (Eve Rodsky, 2019). You then adjust them to your own reality. Full details: the method, in French.

Where do the figures quoted come from?

In the UK, the Office for National Statistics (ONS): women did 60% more unpaid work than men in 2015, 26 hours a week against 16; in 2023, among employed adults aged 18 to 64, 2h 37m of unpaid household work a day for women, 2h 02m for men. Invisible Load Index 2024, 6,000 Europeans surveyed: 86% more time spent thinking about tasks than doing them. The default durations of the tasks come from the French time-use survey (INSEE, 2010), the most detailed task by task: an order of magnitude, to adjust to your household in Fine-tune the result.

Are my answers sent anywhere?

An anonymised copy, yes, and you are told before the result. Everything is calculated in your browser and there is no sign-up: your answers are encoded in the page address, which is the link you share, and the Excel file is generated on your computer. To show the result, the site keeps an anonymised copy for a study on how domestic work is shared: no names, no address, no email, no IP address, nothing that could lead back to you. That is the trade-off for a free, ad-free tool. Fonts are hosted with the site. Audience measurement (Google Analytics) counts visits, only if you accept it in the banner, and never receives your answers.

How long does it take, and how many people?

About ten minutes, together. You enter two first names, in no particular order and with no roles, each person's gender, who is filling in, and the number of children. Tasks go from the most frequent to the rarest, with the house, outdoors and vehicles at the end of the list; durations can be fine-tuned afterwards, below the result.

Can you take stock from ChatGPT or Claude?

Yes. Who thinks of it? also exists as an MCP server, in English and French, which you add as a connector in an assistant (Claude, ChatGPT, VS Code…) with the address https://quiypense-mcp.quiypense-mcp.workers.dev/mcp. The assistant shows the questionnaire in the conversation (or asks the same questions as the site) and gives the same result; first names stay in the conversation and are never passed on. The anonymised copy for the study is announced before the result, as here.

Fine-tune the result

Everything can be corrected here, without going back to the list: who does it, who thinks of it, and the time. The suggested values come from surveys on time spent on domestic worki. For each task: how many times a day, week, month or year, and how many minutes each time (not in total). The daily total recalculates itself. Example: shopping twice a week, 90 minutes each time, makes 26 minutes a day.

The INSEE time-use survey in France (2010), which measures domestic time with diaries, and the Fair Play card deck (Eve Rodsky, 2019) for the tasks the diary does not capture. A couple with children who keeps all the default values totals about eight and a half hours a day, at the high end of what INSEE observes in France. The distinction between ‘thinking of it’ and ‘doing it’ comes from the work of Allison Daminger (2019): anticipating and checking is work, even when someone else carries it out.

A word before you set everything to the minute. Chasing an exact balance line by line wears couples out as much as the imbalance itself. A gap is not a problem as long as it is chosen, talked about and reversible: an imbalance you choose is not an injustice.
